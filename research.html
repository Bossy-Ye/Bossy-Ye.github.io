Research

Quantum computers are becoming engineered systems: programs pass through compilation, mapping, scheduling, and error-corrected execution before they produce a result, and each stage changes what the result means. My research treats this full stack as a software engineering problem. I build toolchains that make the path from problem specification to hardware execution explicit and repeatable, model where fault-tolerant execution actually loses performance, and develop methods for checking that empirical claims about quantum software survive reproduction. The common goal is quantum software whose behaviour, cost, and reported evidence can be trusted.

Quantum workflow generation and hardware-aware execution

C2|Q⟩ is a hardware-agnostic framework that translates classical problem specifications — Python code or structured JSON — into executable quantum workflows. It spans problem modelling, algorithm selection, circuit generation, and hardware recommendation based on fidelity, runtime, and cost. In our evaluation the framework covered ten optimisation problem families and selected appropriate devices for workloads of up to 56 qubits across multiple backends. The work is published in ACM Transactions on Software Engineering and Methodology (TOSEM, 2026) with an ACM-badged reusability and replicability (RCR) report, and is developed within a Business Finland–supported programme on practical quantum software tools.

Fault-tolerant execution under resource constraints

On error-corrected hardware, non-Clifford operations consume magic states, and the rate at which those states can be produced and delivered bounds how fast a circuit can run. This work shows that static T-depth — the metric most compilation passes optimise — does not reliably predict executable performance under bounded T-state delivery. We introduce schedule-level indicators (a slack ratio capturing scheduling flexibility and Δmax, a measure of cumulative demand surplus) that predict slowdown and yield a provable lower bound on executable makespan. The accompanying artifact, BARC, supports backlog-aware analysis of fault-tolerant execution schedules. The paper appears at the IEEE International Conference on Quantum Computing and Engineering (QCE 2026).

Evidence quality and claim stability in quantum software

Empirical quantum-software papers routinely report comparative claims — one compiler produces smaller circuits, one optimiser converges faster, one backend is more efficient. ClaimStab-QC is an audit framework that asks whether such claims are supported by matched evidence exposed in the source paper or artifact, using explicit evidence records, locked audit designs, and scoped stability outcomes. Related empirical work mines developer Q&A platforms for evidence on how quantum software is programmed in practice. This line of work aims to give the field a systematic way to know which reported results remain supported when re-examined.

Ongoing directions

Current work examines synthesis availability across quantum compilation boundaries — how design choices made at one layer of the toolchain constrain what lower layers can still synthesise (manuscript under review) — and HPC-supported exploration for reliable workflow decision-making across heterogeneous classical–quantum infrastructures.

I welcome conversations about collaboration and research visits in these areas, particularly with groups working on fault-tolerant compilation and execution, quantum software toolchains and design automation, and quantum–HPC integration. Funded mobility for a research visit is in place through the University of Oulu's SafeEarth Visitor Programme.

Research

Reliable, resource-aware quantum software systems. A quantum program only becomes a result after passing through translation, compilation, scheduling and error-corrected execution, and every one of those stages changes what the result costs and what it means. I work on three consequences of that: making the path from problem to hardware explicit, predicting where execution actually loses time, and checking whether published comparisons survive the evidence they rest on.

Workflow generation

C2|Q⟩ translates classical problem specifications — Python code or structured JSON — into executable quantum workflows, spanning problem modelling, algorithm selection, circuit generation and hardware recommendation by fidelity, runtime and cost. The evaluation covers ten optimisation problem families and selects appropriate devices for workloads up to 56 qubits across multiple backends. Published in ACM TOSEM with a companion reusability and replicability report.

Execution under resource constraints

On error-corrected hardware, non-Clifford operations consume magic states, and the rate at which those states are produced and delivered bounds how fast a circuit can run. Static T-depth — the metric most compilation passes optimise — does not reliably predict executable performance under bounded delivery. This work introduces schedule-level indicators, a slack ratio capturing scheduling flexibility and Δmax measuring cumulative demand surplus, which predict slowdown and yield a provable lower bound on executable makespan.

Auditing empirical claims

Quantum software papers routinely report that one compiler produces smaller circuits or one optimiser converges faster. ClaimStab-QC asks whether such a claim is supported by matched evidence actually exposed in its source paper or artifact: it records baselines, metric, relation and admissible evidence, locks the comparison design before outcomes are computed, and returns either a scoped result or an explicit evidence boundary. Applied to 455 comparative claims drawn from 119 papers, it exposes a substantial gap between claims made and evidence available to support them.

Ongoing exploration

Current work examines how compilation decisions constrain what later stages can still synthesise: circuits that are formally equivalent can differ in the synthesis choices that remain available to them downstream. I am also beginning to look at distributed quantum computing and the middleware layer between classical and quantum resources, where the scheduling and resource questions above reappear across machines rather than within one. This direction is exploratory and has no completed results yet.
